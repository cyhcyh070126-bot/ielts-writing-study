展开审题、观点与写法

不同角度怎样展开

这些是备选论证方向，不是唯一正确立场。选择后要回应题目范围。

  • 支持限制：减少市中心拥堵和路边停车，为步行、公交和公共空间腾出位置。
  • 反对全面禁止：部分行动不便者或夜间通勤者缺少替代交通，禁令可能使他们难以获得基本服务。
  • 可比较的条件：公交覆盖、例外许可及实施范围；解释为何限制部分车辆不同于禁止全部车辆。

本题审题与一种计划

  • 抓住all和city centres：题目提出的是全面禁止，不只是适度限制。
  • 说明赞成哪些限制、反对哪些绝对化安排。
  • 承认车辆限制的收益，同时解释需要保留的例外和配套条件。

同意限制日常私家车通行；反对立即、无例外地全面禁行，公共交通和必要出行保障应先到位。

  • 引言：支持减少车辆，但不同意无条件全面禁行。
  • 主体一：说明减少普通车辆能改善空气、公交效率与步行环境。
  • 主体二：说明行动不便者和必要出行的困难，提出条件与例外。
  • 结论：概括有条件限制的立场。

论证链

  • 减少普通通勤车辆 → 公交更准时、道路空间释放 → 步行骑行环境改善 → 更多人有条件放弃开车。
  • 全面禁行忽视行动能力与夜间交通差异 → 必要出行受阻 → 设置明确豁免并完善公交 → 保留减排收益同时减少不公平。

易错点

  • 写partly agree，却不说明同意与反对的边界。
  • 忽略all，整篇只支持适度限行，未评价全面禁行。
  • 提出例外后不解释为何这些例外不等于放弃减排目标。
句框 · 按内容填槽

引言

There are good reasons to [less absolute version of the proposal]. However, I only partly agree that [the full proposal], because [the limit or exception that matters].

部分同意要明确边界，不能只写两面都有道理。

主体一

I support [the part you accept] because [reason]. [Explain the mechanism]. For example, [specific situation]. This would [immediate effect] and [further effect]. Therefore, [state precisely which part of the policy is justified].

写清楚你赞成的范围，例如日常通勤车辆，而不是悄悄改写题目。

主体二

Nevertheless, extending this policy to [the part you reject] would create difficulties. [Explain who would be affected and how]. For instance, [concrete exception]. A better approach would be to [condition or limited alternative]. This would preserve [benefit] without [avoidable cost].

反对的部分需要理由与具体对象；例外和配套应与困难对应。

结论

In conclusion, I favour [accepted part], provided that [necessary condition]. I do not support [rejected absolute version].

结尾同时交代赞成什么、限制在哪里。

完整原创范文

Restricting private cars can make crowded city centres cleaner and more pleasant. However, I only partly agree with a complete ban, because reducing unnecessary traffic is desirable while preventing every essential car journey would be unfair and impractical.

There are strong reasons to remove ordinary commuter traffic from the busiest streets. When fewer cars compete for limited road space, buses can move more reliably and pedestrians have less exhaust pollution to breathe. Councils can also turn some parking areas into wider pavements, trees or safe cycle routes. For example, a shopping street served by frequent buses could restrict car access during business hours, allowing customers to arrive without filling the surrounding roads with parked vehicles. Such changes would make choosing public transport easier while improving the experience of people who already walk or cycle.

Nevertheless, an absolute ban would ignore differences in people's needs and the quality of local transport. A resident with limited mobility may be unable to walk from a distant station, while a worker finishing a late shift may have no practical bus connection. These journeys should not be treated as equivalent to someone driving a short distance merely for convenience. Cities should therefore improve evening services, provide accessible transport and allow clearly defined exemptions before introducing wider restrictions. A gradual system of restricted zones and permits would still discourage unnecessary driving without making the centre inaccessible to people who depend on a car. Success should be measured by access as well as traffic reduction.

In conclusion, I support substantial restrictions on private cars where reliable alternatives exist, but a blanket ban should be replaced by a policy that includes necessary exemptions and better public transport.
